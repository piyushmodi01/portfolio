<Pull>
  When complexity becomes invisible, people stop questioning it. They absorb it and call it the way things work here.
</Pull>

<Prose>
The tools that engineering and operations teams depend on rarely get the same design attention as consumer products. They accumulate, patch by patch, workaround by workaround, until the cognitive overhead of doing a basic task becomes genuinely obstructive. Nobody files a bug for "this is just hard." They adapt. They build muscle memory around the friction.

With tens of thousands of internal users across workflows spanning buildings, documents, real-estate operations, and engineering data, that friction has a real cost. Hours lost per week, per person. Decisions made on stale information. Teams building shadow systems in spreadsheets because the official tool doesn't surface what they need.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="02"
  title="The Work"
  intro="Good design at this scale is mostly alignment work. The interface is the last 20%."

<Prose>
The first thing I did was stop designing. I spent the first weeks understanding, sitting in on team standups, mapping information flows, understanding which parts of the workflow were load-bearing and which were artifacts of an older system architecture.

The existing UI reflected how the system had been built, not how people worked. Systems get designed by engineers who understand the data model, not by anyone who has watched a person navigate it at 9am under deadline pressure.
</Prose>

<Pull accent>
  The best thing a design system does isn't make everything look the same. It makes every team faster at the decisions that matter, and eliminates the decisions that don't.
</Pull>

<Prose>
One of the more interesting workstreams involved designing AI-enabled surfaces, specifically how to embed contextual AI assistance into tools that engineers use to navigate complex data.

The challenge with AI in enterprise tools isn't the technology. It's the trust model. People working with high-stakes operational data are appropriately skeptical of opaque outputs. The design work here was largely about making the AI's reasoning visible: showing what it used to arrive at an answer, making it easy to verify, and making it easy to dismiss when it's wrong.
</Prose>

<MarqueeStrip text="Enterprise UX · Design Systems · AI-Enabled Workflows · Internal Tools · Interaction Design · Systems Thinking · " />

<Decision label="Critical decision">
  <span>Design the system layer before the product layer. Tokens, components, and interaction patterns established upfront so that individual product decisions compound instead of conflict.</span>
</Decision>

<Callout variant="insight">
  The teams using these tools are deeply technical. Designing for that audience means trusting them with more information, not less, and giving them controls they'll actually use.
</Callout>

  The biggest shift working at this scale is learning when to stop designing and start aligning. Sometimes the best artefact I can make is a clear decision, not a frame in Sketch.

<Prose>
I'd push into engineering conversations earlier. The constraints they're working with often reveal better design directions than any framework exercise. I'd also invest more time early in the user feedback loop: at this scale, the distance between designer and user can become wide fast.
