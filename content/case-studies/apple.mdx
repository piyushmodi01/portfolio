<Chapter
  number="01"
  title="The Environment"
  intro="Most products fight for a user's time. This one is part of how thousands of employees do their jobs every day."
/>

<Prose>
I designed an internal platform used daily by thousands of employees to manage real estate, track facilities, and handle operations across a global footprint. I'm embedded in the product team, working alongside a single engineering lead and several PMs running parallel projects at the same time.

The people using these tools are colleagues doing real work. That shifts the frame. There's no marketing, no onboarding funnel, no star rating. You earn trust by being genuinely useful, consistently.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="02"
  title="The System I Build"
  intro="Every product in this org builds on the Globe Design System. One designer's decisions in every surface."
/>

<Pull accent>
  When you own the token layer, a single design decision reflects through everything built on top of it.
</Pull>

<Prose>
Globe Design System is the shared foundation for this org. I design and maintain its token layer: the type ramp, the spacing scale, the full semantic color model across light and dark mode. Every product, every screen, and every component inherits these decisions.

218 typography tokens. 84 semantic color tokens. 7 breakpoints. These aren't numbers to cite. They're the surface area of a single contribution. A token decision made in Globe affects everything downstream, all at once. Getting it right the first time is the job.

It means specifying why a value is what it is, documenting what changes and what shouldn't, and holding that standard across every team that builds from it. Every exception outside the system is a decision that will eventually fracture consistency.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="03"
  title="The Process I Introduced"
  intro="Design and engineering were in separate lanes. Sprint structure changed that."
/>

<Prose>
Before the sprint format was introduced, design and engineering operated with a gap between them. Specs arrived, builds happened, mismatches were caught late. I introduced a 5-day sprint structure that closed that gap by keeping both disciplines in the same room from the start.
</Prose>

  eyebrow="How the sprint works"

<Chapter
  title="A Tool Built for Design, Adopted by Everyone"
  intro="Accessibility has always been design's responsibility. The goal was to make it everyone's habit."
/>

<Prose>
The Accessibility Checker started as a personal tool — a way to run contrast checks and accessibility audits against internal standards rather than generic WCAG benchmarks alone. I built it as a browser extension, a Sketch plugin, and a bookmarklet so it could run inside whatever workflow someone was already in.

What happened next wasn't planned: PMs started using it before design reviews. Engineers started running it before code review. The tool was available at the right moment in the right workflows, so the behavior changed.
</Prose>

<Pull accent>
  Accessibility is design's job. The tool just made it easier for everyone else to participate.
</Pull>

<Prose>
Version 1.1.0. Adopted by design, PM, and engineering teams across the org. The checker made a responsibility that lived primarily in design into a habit shared across the whole team.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="05"
  title="What Shipped"
  intro="Seven products running in parallel. All NDA. All shipped or handed to engineering."
/>

<Prose>
Seven products shipped or handed to engineering, all NDA. One worth describing without showing:

One of the platforms had a significant navigation problem — the right information existed, but the people who needed it couldn't find it without asking someone who already knew where to look. The constraint wasn't technical. The data was there. The problem was a UI that had been extended over time without ever being reconsidered as a whole.

I ran usability sessions, mapped where people actually looked versus where the information lived, and redesigned the structure around the real workflow — not the assumed one. The fix was simpler than anyone expected. It usually is.

The other six products follow similar patterns. I'm happy to walk through any of them.
</Prose>
