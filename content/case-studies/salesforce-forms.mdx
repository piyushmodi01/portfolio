<Chapter
  number="01"
  title="The Problem"
  intro="Government data collection sounds like an IT problem. It isn't."
/>

<Prose>
US government programs depend on forms. Benefits applications, child welfare assessments, case management intake, eligibility screening: the form is often the first interaction between a person in need and the system that's supposed to help them. The form has to be accurate, because a wrong answer has real consequences. It has to handle complexity, because government policy is never simple. And it has to work for the person filling it out, whether that's a citizen on a slow connection or a case worker interviewing a family.

Deloitte was building these forms custom for every client engagement, each one starting from a blank Salesforce project, each one needing its own development, testing, and maintenance. Nothing carried over. Everything took longer than it should.

The opportunity: build one platform that any Deloitte team could use to configure and deploy forms for any government program, without writing a single line of code each time.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="02"
  title="What We Built"
  intro="The platform needed two entry modes, because the same form serves two very different people."
/>

<Prose>
The first version of DCF centred on a Conversational UI: one question at a time, linear, clean. For the right use case, it was genuinely good: data quality was high, users stayed focused, and shorter forms felt effortless. Several clients adopted it and it worked well for them.

But it was built around an assumption: that the person filling out the form could answer each question before seeing the next one. For shorter citizen-facing forms, that assumption held. For complex, multi-page government assessments, it didn't.
</Prose>

<Tension
  beforeLabel="Conversational UI"
  beforeTitle="One question at a time. High data quality. Right for shorter, simpler forms."
  beforeBody="Works when the user can answer sequentially without needing context from later questions. Great for online citizen forms. Fails for complex assessments where the person filling it out needs the full picture."
  afterLabel="Traditional UI"
  afterTitle="Full form visible. Progress tracked. Built for people who need to plan the conversation, not just answer it."
  afterBody="Designed for case workers, intake specialists, and anyone who needs to navigate a complex form while also managing a human conversation on the other side of the table."
/>

<Callout variant="insight">
  Both modes shipped. Every form in the platform has a toggle: case workers and citizens can switch between Conversational and Traditional based on how they work. The use case decides, not the designer.
</Callout>

<Chapter
  number="03"
  title="Where It Broke"
  intro="The moment a client meeting ends badly is the moment you learn something you couldn't have learned any other way."
/>

<Pull>
  The State of Indiana told us the product wouldn't work in their state. Their case workers sit across from children. They cannot fill out a form one question at a time.
</Pull>

<Prose>
We were demoing the Conversational UI to what we believed was the State of Indiana. The goal was to show them how quickly we could configure and deploy their intake forms. We thought we'd sold it.

Their feedback was immediate: their case workers work in the field, meeting children and families in difficult situations. They need to see the whole form so they can plan the conversation. What questions are coming, what sections are next, how much is left. Asking one question, waiting, then asking the next would break the conversation. The meeting ended.

That moment changed what we built. It wasn't a failure of the feature. It was a failure of understanding who was in the room. Case workers aren't filling out a form. They're conducting a conversation while a form happens to be open.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="04"
  title="The Redesign"
  intro="Developers had already built a traditional UI. Without design involved. It showed."
/>

<Prose>
The dev team had moved quickly after the Indiana meeting and built a version of the traditional UI on their own: multiple columns, no clear flow, no visual hierarchy. It was functional in the way that a spreadsheet is functional.

I came in, rebuilt it from scratch around a single question: what does a case worker actually need when they look at the screen?

The answer, from watching day-in-the-life videos of case workers: as little as possible, as clearly as possible. Case workers are sitting across from people in difficult situations. Every second they look at the screen is a second they're not looking at the person. When they do look at the screen, they need to understand their position instantly. Where they are, what's next, how much is left. So they can plan without losing the thread of the conversation.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="05"
  title="What It Became"
/>

<Prose>
I never met a single user. The chain from me to the families filling out these forms ran five layers deep: India office, US office, state government, case worker, citizen. I designed for people I only knew through videos and secondhand notes. The platform is still running, still being expanded, still being rolled out to new clients. I found that out from colleagues after I'd already left.
</Prose>
