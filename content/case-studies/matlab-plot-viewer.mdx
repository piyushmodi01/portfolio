<Chapter
  number="01"
  title="The World"
  intro="Before the problem, the context. Without it, none of the decisions make sense."
/>

<Prose>
MATLAB is used by engineers and scientists at Tesla, NASA, aerospace companies, and research labs worldwide. It's where they analyse simulation data, compare test results, and make sense of complex outputs. That work produces plots (visual representations of data), and engineers generate dozens of them in a single session.

The standard experience: run the code, watch new plot windows appear on screen, each one floating separately, stacking on top of each other. To compare two plots, you drag and resize windows by hand. Run the code again and every arrangement is gone. Start over.

It's not a dramatic problem. Nobody files a ticket saying "this is broken." It's just slow, repetitive, and accepted as part of the job.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="02"
  title="The Problem"
  intro="MathWorks had already built a better way. Almost nobody knew it existed."
/>

<Prose>
The engineering team had already built a fix: Tab and Tile View. All plots in one window as tabs, one click to arrange them all into a grid. No floating windows, no manual rearranging.

Before Beta, a researcher gave 7 engineers one task: find the feature that arranges all your plots into a grid view. Five minutes. Only 1 found it. The rest gave up or asked for hints.

The feature was buried in a 3-dot menu under the label "Tile All." That phrase meant nothing to someone looking for a grid arrangement. One label. Two completely different mental models.
</Prose>

<Pull>
  When the researcher finally showed them the feature, every single engineer said the same thing: this would save me so much time.
</Pull>

<Prose>
The feature worked. Users loved it the moment they saw it. But the problem was never just the label. Even engineers who found "Tile All" hit a second wall: a submenu with radio buttons and a manual grid picker - Single, Left/Right, Top/Bottom, then a 5x5 grid of squares to choose a layout. Like inserting a table in Word. Nobody connected that to "arrange my plots." The label sent them to the wrong place, and the interaction confirmed they were lost. Both problems needed fixing.
</Prose>

<Chapter
  number="03"
  title="The Work"
  intro="Every obvious solution hit a wall. The real design started when the obvious options ran out."
/>

<Prose>
Before exploring solutions, I studied how other tools handle tabbed layouts and view switching: VS Code, Figma, Chrome DevTools, Power BI, Tableau. The goal was to understand what patterns engineers already knew before proposing anything new.
</Prose>

<Prose>
The study surfaced more than just discoverability. Engineers were also losing their tab layout every time they closed the window - the only way to reopen it was to re-run the code. There were performance questions around how the tab view handled large numbers of plots. And there were smaller workflow improvements worth exploring, all specific to the new tab and tile layout. More problems than one sprint could hold.

So before any design work started, I ran an Impact-Effort Matrix with the researcher, PM, and engineering lead. Every candidate improvement on the board, every voice in the room. The scope decision needed to be shared, not handed down. "Quick switching between views" landed in High Impact, Low Effort. Unanimous. Everything else moved to the backlog.
</Prose>

<Prose>
I mapped every way to make the feature more visible and accessible. Each direction had a clear rationale. Each one failed for a concrete, documented reason.
</Prose>

<StackedCards
  eyebrow="What was tried, and why it didn't work"
  c1num="01" c1title="Put it on the taskbar" c1body="Highest visibility, one click. Business said no: the taskbar is shared across all of MATLAB. A plot-specific view toggle doesn't belong in a global space used by every part of the product."
  c2num="02" c2title="Add a toggle button inside the menu" c2body="Visually communicates two states better than any other component. The codebase said no: MATLAB has been running since the 1980s. A toggle component simply didn't exist in the system. You can't build what the platform can't support."
  c3num="03" c3title="A view switcher in the top ribbon" c3body="Most visible real estate in the product. Cross-team dependencies said no: any change to the top ribbon requires approval from every team across MATLAB. The scope was impossible for a single-feature fix."
/>

<Pull accent>
  If dev can't build any of these, what can we actually do?
</Pull>

<Prose>
That question reframed everything. I stopped asking what the ideal solution looked like and started asking what was achievable within the constraints: the 1980s codebase, the design system's existing components, and the patterns MATLAB users already knew.

The answer was a checkbox. Not glamorous, but a checkbox was already used throughout MATLAB's menus. Engineers knew how to read it. It fit the design system. The codebase could support it. Check it and the system picks the best grid layout automatically. No more choosing between 2x3 and 3x3. No more manual configuration.
</Prose>

<Decision label="The decision that defined the redesign">
  <span>System decides the optimal layout. The user just says yes or no. No more choosing grid configurations, no more manual arrangement.</span>
</Decision>

<Callout variant="constraint">
  A checkbox was the right answer because it was the one component that worked within a codebase from the 1980s, matched the design system's rules for context menus, and fit the mental model MATLAB users already had. Designing within constraints isn't a compromise. It's reading the room.
</Callout>

<Chapter
  number="04"
  title="What Changed"
/>

<Prose>
The redesign shipped. One checkbox. The system picks the layout. Engineers who spent 35 to 45 seconds manually arranging plots were doing it in 5 to 8. The feature that 6 out of 7 engineers couldn't find became the first thing they reached for.

When I presented the final design, the developers who had maintained that part of MATLAB for years were the ones who reacted first. Not the PM, not the researcher. The people closest to the code. They knew what the users had been dealing with - and they knew this fixed it. That reaction meant more than any test result.
</Prose>
